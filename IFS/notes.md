- **Academy Ratio** - In the silent era, the ratio of width and height
  of the film frames was 1.33:1 (4:3). This changed once the sound was
  added and the frames became more squared (1.37:1) sound to no sound
  height. This ratio was standardized by the Academy of Motion Picture
  Arts thus called Academy ratio.

- **Actualities** - Older term used for documentary films

- **Anamorphic lens** - A lens for making widescreen films in the
  academic ratio. The anamorphic lens captures the wideview footage and
  squeezes it into a film frame, the frame is then projected using a
  projector lens which displays the content in the on a wide theatre
  screen. CinemaScope and Panavision are some common anamorphic
  widescreen processes.

- **Animation** - Any form of artificial motion that is created by
  photographing a series of drawings, images or objects. The small
  changes in position and size creates an illusion of motion.

- **Art Cinema**

  1.  A critical term used to describe movies which were filmed in
      commercial circumstances but the style of filming was inspired by
      "modernist trends" of the "high art", which was different from the
      mainstream cinema.
  2.  In american film industry used to describe imported films which
      were catered for the upper-middle class or the college-educated
      class of audience.

- **Artisanal Production** - The process of filmmaking where the
  producer, crew involved spend their energy on a film without
  expectation of future collaborations. This term is used in contrast to
  mass produced films with division of labour.

- **Aspect Ratio** - The proportion of width and height. The academy
  aspect ratio was similar to the film frame's aspect ratio (4:3), but
  later more widescreen formats started being used like 16:9.

- **Auteur** - The "author" of the film, usually the director or the
  person responsible for the film. It was also used for discribing good
  directors and in contrast to the bad ones. This term's use became
  controversial during the post world war-II french and english movies
  because of how the movie was seen as auteur's own work or property.

- **Avance sur recettes** - (Advance on receipts) is a government loan
  which is given to a film based on the anticipated ticket sales. This
  model was popularized by the French Government and started similar was
  adopted in other countries.

- **Axis of Action** - In continuity editing, An imaginary line that
  passes between the actors in a scene, which creates a spatial
  continuity of the objects and their relative positions to be left or
  right. The camera is not supposed to cross this line inverting the
  spatial positions. Also called the 180 degree line.

- **Backlighting** - Illumination casted on the figure in a scene from a
  light source which is located on the opposite side of the camera. It
  usually creates a thin outline of light on the back of the figure.

- **Block Booking** - A practice in which the distributor sells the
  films to the exhibitor in a block of several films. These other
  possibly undesirable films are forced to be purchased in order to get
  one or two high priority desired film. This practice was standard in
  early 1910s but was finally outlawed in 1960s under the "paramount
  decision".

- **Camera Angle** - It is the position of the frame relative to the
  objects in the scene. If the camera is above, pointing down it is a
  called high angle. If the camera is horizontal and at the same level
  it is called Straight on. If the camera is below, pointing above it is
  called low angle.

- **Camera Movement** - The onscreen impression of movement of the frame
  with respect to the objects in the scene. Usually done by moving the
  camera itself, or by zooming the camera lens, or sometimes even
  special effects.

- **Catented frame** - The type of framing in which the sides are not
  levelled. Either the left or the right side is lower, showing the
  figures to be tipped.

- **Cel Animation** - The type of animation which animates series of
  drawings on a celluloid or cel. The small changes in position of the
  figure gives it an illusion of motion.

- **Cinematography** - A general term used to describe all the
  modifications made to the film during the filming process to the
  camera positions cuts and to the post processing done in the
  laboratory.

- **Close-up** - A framing process in which the figures (actors or
  objects) in the shot are close to the camera and are large. Usually
  for actors, it captures the face, from the neck to the top of the
  head. Captures medium sized objects.

- **Collage** - A film made by assembling footage from sources which are
  widely disparate. These contain collections of artificially recorded
  scenes, animations, newsreels and public footage.

- **Compilaiton film** - A genre of documentary films which constists of
  collection of footage from public press, which tries to capture a
  large scale movement or war. Widely used in government or
  public-affairs documentaries.

- **Continuity Editing** - The system of cutting which each cut has a
  spatial and temporal similarity which gives it an impression of being
  continuous. It is based on the anticipation of the viewer to what is
  supposed to be shown next. It is achieved by having the two shots
  having similar sequence of actions, relative positioning of objects,
  and same background setting.

- **Crane Shot** - A shot in which the framing seems off-the ground,
  frame is moved above or below vertically or laterally in the air. This
  is usually done by mounting the camera to a crane.

- **Cross Cutting** - Style of cutting in which two subjects are shown
  on screen switching them back and forth. Usually done when there is a
  conversation or any interation between the two subjects.

- **Cut** -

  1.  In filmmaking it is the process of joining two films together with
      a splice
  2.  In final film, it refers to an immediate switch from one shot to
      another.

- **Cut In** - A cut where a larger shot capturing a lot of info is cut
  to a closer shot, which is a small portion of the previous shot.

- **Cycle** - In cinema history, it refers to a short-lived span of time
  where a particular subgenre of a film is trending or is fashion. Eg:
  "adult" Westeners in 1950s or the Heroic Gangsters in the 1980s.

- **DeDramatization** - It is a process of minimizing the suspense,
  highly emotional scenes, and physically action heavy scenes, in favour
  of low-key subtle things requiring more emphasis like character
  portayal, surroundings, storyline. It was used primarily in european
  cinema post world-war II era, where environment, and character
  portrayal, was given more emphasis..

- **Deep focus** - The use of camera lens and lighting in such a way
  that both the planes closer and farther away from camera are in focus.

- **Deep space** - It is an arrangement of mise-en-scene elements such
  that the there is large distance between planes closest to the camera
  and planes furthest away from the camera. Any or all of these elements
  maybe in focus

- **Depth of Field** - It is the measure of distance of the closest
  plane to the camera which is in focus and the plance furthest from the
  camera which is in focus. If the Depth of Field is 6m to 15m, elements
  which are before 6m are out of focus and beyond 15m are out of focus.

- **Diegetic Sound** - Any music, voice, or sound effect that is shown
  as originating from within the film's world or the Diegesis.

- **Diorama** - It is a 19th century form of entertainment in which,
  audience are seated in a ciruclar room and stare at a large
  transparent painting with moving objects and lighting.

- **Direct Sound** - The sound that is recorded during the filming, by
  the equiment in the studio. This is in contrast to the
  post-synchronous sound. This was popular in the early sound era and
  Direct cinema documentaries.

- **Dissolve** - A type of transition in a cut where ones shot fades
  away slowly while other appears, in the end the latter shot completely
  appears and former disappears. During the transition, the two shots
  superimpose each other.

- **Distance of Framing** - It is the position and closeness of the
  camera relative to the mise-en-scene elements. Also called camera
  distance or shot size.

- **Distribution** - One of the three branches of film industry. It is
  the process of supplying the films to the exhibitors or the places
  where they will be shown.

- **Dolly** - A movable camera which is usually attached to a trolley on
  a track. Used for making tracking shots.

- **Dubbing** - The process in which all of the voices and sounds
  recorded in the film are rerecorded for corrections or translations.

- **Editing** -

  1.  In filmmaking the task of selecting and joinining camera takes.
  2.  In finished film, the set of techniques that governs the relation
      among shots.

- **Ellipsis** - In narrative, the process of omitissions of certain
  portions of actions.

- **Elliptical Editing** - A style of editing where in certain scenes
  the actions are omitted in such a way to startle or confuse the viewer
  about the missing stretches.

- **Establishing shot** - Usually involving distant framing, to
  encapsulate all the elements of the scene, their spatial relation and
  all the important figures, objects and settings in a scene.

- **Exhibition** - On of the branches of film industry, the process of
  streaming or exhibiting the film onto a wide screen to an audience.

- **Experimental Cinema** - Filmmaking which avoids the conventional
  mainstream material as subject matter and addresses not unpopular or
  taboo/suppressed subject matter. These types of films avoid narrative
  forms, exploring lyric, associational, descriptive or other formal
  means.

- **Extreme Close up** - Framing which enlarges a small detail, like a
  body part or a small object, or text.

- **Eyeline Match** - A cut obeying axis of action principle, where the
  first scene which has a subject looking at something is followed by
  the object that the subject looks at, matching the eyeline of the
  subject. If the subject looks at something towards the left off
  screen, the shot following has the object on the right of the screen.

- **Fade** - It is a transition effect where shots appear or disappear
  from a solid color background. Fade-In refers to the shot appearing
  gradually from usually a black background, used to open a film or a
  chapter. Fade-Out refers to the shot disappearing gradually into a
  solid color usually white (death or dream) or black.

- **Fill Light** - A type of lighting, other than the key lighting,
  which fills in the dark shawdows caused by the key lighting and
  softens it, usually placed on the side other than the key light's
  source.

- **Film Noir** - (Dark film) A term used by french critics to describe
  american films to characterize the genres like detective, or thriller
  with low key or less lighting with somber mood. Film noir was most
  prevelant in the 1940s and 1950s after which it had its revival.

- **Film Stock** - A long material which onto which the film frames are
  casted. It contains clear base on one side, with light sensitive
  emulsion.

- **Filter** - It is a transparent glass or gelatin which is placed in
  front of the camera lens or the printer lens, which is used to control
  the amount of light casted on the frames.

- **Flashbacks** - A form of narration where sequence of actions which
  are occuring at the present are followed by actions that happened in
  the past.

- **Flashforward** - A form of narration in which the storyline is
  interrupted with sequences from the future and switch back to the
  present.

- **Focal Length** - The distance from the camera lens at which the
  light focusses with maximum sharpness. The focal length of the camera
  lens determines the perspective relations of the space represented on
  flat screen.

- **Focus** - The degree to which the light rays coming into the lens
  are able to converge sharply on the film frame. High focus creates
  sharp outlines and distinct textures.

- **Frame** - It is a strip which contains the singular stationary image
  captured by the camera lens. When projected onto the screen with quick
  succession it forms an animation.

- **Framing** - It is using the edges of the frame to select and compose
  what finally reaches the film.

- **Front Projection** - It is a composite filming procedure which
  involves a footage being projected on a screen to use as background,
  while the elements in the foreground are enacted and filmed by a
  camera placed in the front.

- **Frontality** - In staging, it is a measure of how much the actors or
  subjects face the viewers.

- **Gauge** - It is the measure of width of a film frame measured in
  millimeters. Standard gauges are 8mm, 16mm, 35mm, 70mm.

- **Genres** - It is a general categorization of films or cinema based
  on repeated conventions in both storylines and cinematography. Common
  genres are horror, westerns, detective.

- **Graphic Match** - A style of cutting where the two shots have high
  similarity in compositional elements of the scene. It can be color or
  shape.

- **Hand-held Camera** - The style of filming where the camera operator
  physically holds or supports the camera by either using their hands or
  using a harness. It was rare during the silent era but started being
  used in 1940s and 1950s and is widely used in direct documentaries.

- **Hard Lighting** - Illumination which results in high contrast and
  casts sharp shadows.

- **Height of framing** - The height of the camera above the ground,
  regardless of the angle.

- **High-key Lighting** - Style of lighting which has low contrast
  between less lit and high lit sides and shadows and dark areas are
  softened by large amounts of fill light. Shadows are transparent and
  all areas are visible.

- **Horizontal Integration** - In the film industry, the term is used
  when the corporations try to scale within a sector. Ex: if a
  production company tries to buy up other production firms.

- **Intellectual Montage** - A style of editing which juxtapositions
  images to create an intellectual purpose or a meaning, which the
  individual images can't deliver. It was popularized by Russian
  directors like Eisenstein and widespread used in experimental films.

- **Intercutting** - In narrative, the process in which two or more
  sequences of action are shown following each other switching back and
  forth among them. These sequences of action are shown to usually take
  in a different place simultaneously.

- **Iris** - It is a small circular opening at a portion of the screen.
  It is used as a transition for ending or starting films. Iris-Out is
  usually used towards the end of the film, emphasizing on a particular
  object in the frame before cutting out. Iris-In on the other hand is
  used to emphasis an object followed by revealing the surroundings in
  the next shot.

- **Jump Cut** - An elliptical cut between two shots where there is an
  abrupt movement between the two shots. A jump cut usually either
  consists of the elements in the front remaining same and the
  background switching, or the background staying constant while the
  elements in the front change.

- **Key Light** - In three point lighting, it is the highest intensity
  illumination, coming on to a scene.

- **Linearity** - In narrative, it is a degree to which the story
  delivers the line of action directly, without much distractions and
  delays. Basically a measure of how straight to the point the narration
  gets.

- **Long Shot** - A type of framing where the elements in the scene
  appear small. This framing contains the whole person in the shot while
  revealing more info about the surroundings.

- **Long Take** - A long uninterrupted shot, which is unusually longer
  than a regular take. It was rare in the silent era but raised in
  popularity in 1930s and 1940s.

- **Low-Key Lighting** - The style of lighting where there is high
  contrast between high lit and low lit elements and gives a relatively
  dark aesthetic and contains sharp shadows without much fill light.

- **Majors** - In film industry the term used to refer to the major
  companies in the film production industry in the US. In the 1920s the
  Big Three companies were popular which later turned into Big Five and
  now it contains other smaller production-distribution companies.

- **Mask** - It is a black covering which in the camera or the printer
  which covers the light falling on the film. It is usually black but
  also exists in other colors like white.

- **Masking** - It is the material canvas or fabric which forms as a
  theater screen onto which projectors project the film on. Usually
  black in color.

- **Match on Action** - It is a style of cutting, where the two shots
  have similar gesture. It creates a continuity and completeness of an
  action in a scene.

- **Matte Shot** - A type of process shot where two images photographed
  from different occasions are combined together to make one image, in a
  laboratory using post processing techniques.

- **Medium shot** - Type of framing where the objects in the frame are
  not too far or close from the camera. Usually for a person, it starts
  from the waist to head level, capturing the upper body.

- **Minors** - In US Film industry in 1920s to 1950s these were
  significant companies which were not as big as Majors and are
  production firms that didn't own theaters. These were also called the
  'little three'

- **mise-en-scene** - These consist of all elements of the scene which
  are present in front of the camera including settings, props,
  costumes, makeup, lighting etc.

- **Mixing** - Combing two or more sound tracks by rerecording them and
  combining them into single track.

- **Montage** - Synonymous to the word editing in french. But it is used
  to describe an editing style emerging in 1920s Soviet Film making,
  which combined sequence of dynamic and discontinuous images. Usually
  Intellectual montage which conveys an abstract idea.

- **Montage Sequence** - The segment of film which compresses the
  passage of time into loosely related events using symbolic or typical
  images. Frequently uses dissolves, fades, wipes and other transition
  effects.

- **Motif** - It is an element in a film which is repeated throughout
  the film

- **Movement** - In film industry it is a process in which, filmmakers
  who belong to the same era or location sharing a distincitve opinion
  on how films should be made. Typically these movies shared common
  stylistic and thematic elements in narration and cinematorgraphy. Some
  popular movements in film industry are French Surrealism in 1920s.
  French New Wave in 1950s which was loose.

- **Nickeloadean Boom** - Beggining in 1905, the rapid rise of
  small-scale store-front theaters. Which ended in 1910s in favour of
  big theaters.

- **Non-Diegetic Sound** - Sound which is added to the film which is not
  a part of the film's world. Like Narration or Moody music.

- **Normal lens** - A lens which is used to photogram images not to
  sharply, but also not having large depth of field. Typically in the
  range of 35mm to 50mm.

- **180-degree System** - A system of continuity editing where in a
  scene all shots must follow similar directionality, so that relative
  positions of the elements are to the left or right. The 180 degree
  line is same as line of axis,

- **Overlapping editing** - A style of editing where a particular action
  is repated multiple times, increasing the action's screen time.
  Usually done with different camera angles.

- **Pan** - When the camera swivels its way from left to right. In the
  film it scans the space horizontally.

- **Pan and Zoom** - An alternative to the Cut-in and Cut-out technique,
  which is used to garner attention to a particular portion of the
  scene. Usually used in long shots where cuts are not allowed. These
  became popular in the 1950s and 1960s when long shots became popular
  in the first place.

- **Pixillation** - A style of filming where the subjects (usually
  person), moves in immediate stoac of bursts, which is made by cutting
  together various jump shots.

- **Plan-sequence** - (sequence shot) A french word to describe a shot
  that captures the whole scene. Usually a long shot.

- **POV (point of view)** - A type of framing where the camera is seemed
  to be position at the subject's eye level looking at what the subject
  is supposed to see. It is followed or preceded by a shot of subject
  looking off the frame.

- **Post Synchronization** - It is a process in which audio is added to
  a film that was already shot and assembled. Usually for adding
  diegetic sounds or dubbing. Most of the contemporary fiction movies
  are post synchronized.

- **Process shot** - Any shot that involves combining two or more images
  together to create a composite image or a special effect.

- **Protectionism** - The set of policies by the government to protects
  is national film makers from imported films. These include subsidies
  for production, limiting the number of imported films, and devoting
  proportion of time for casting of local films.

- **Racking Focus** - When the focus is shifted from one plane to other
  plane within a shot. Inside the film it is called "rack focus"
